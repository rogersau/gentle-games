# Facilitator Protocol

## Before The Session

- Include autistic children and caregivers in recruitment and design decisions. Invite educators and therapists as design partners when useful, without treating them as substitutes for children's views.
- Confirm caregiver consent and child assent using [consent and stop criteria](consent-and-stop.md). Assent is ongoing, not a one-time signature.
- Check the child's preferred communication, input method, sensory settings, breaks, support person, and words or signals for "stop". Do not require eye contact, stillness, speech, or a particular self-regulation style.
- Use a quiet, familiar space, a charged device, and the smallest suitable test set from the [device and input matrix](device-input-matrix.md).
- Prepare one neutral task card per game. Do not preview a "right" way to play.
- Use an anonymous session code only. Keep consent documentation and any recordings outside this repository in the approved, access-controlled study location.

## Session Flow

1. **Welcome and choice:** Explain that this is a test of the game, not the child. The child may choose a game, pause, skip, change settings, take a break, or leave. Ask where they would like to sit and whether they want the caregiver nearby.
2. **Independent comprehension:** Show the game with no adult explanation. Ask, "What do you think you can do here?" Accept pointing, touching, gesture, AAC, sign, drawing, silence, or words. Record what the interface communicated, not an ability judgement.
3. **Replay and channels:** Let the child replay the instruction. Offer the available text, visual, spoken, and control-based channels in the same order the product offers them. Do not add an adult paraphrase until the planned support step.
4. **Start, pause, stop, skip, leave:** Ask the child to find or use each available autonomy control. Never make leaving depend on completion. If a control is absent, record that as a finding rather than supplying it through the facilitator.
5. **Support ladder:** Use the least support first, then fade it as soon as the child shows they can continue. Record the exact support and whether the child initiated it.
6. **New example transfer:** After one example, present a genuinely new in-game example with changed item, colour, position, or pattern. Do not repeat the answer or coach the rule. Look for independent use of the instruction, not speed or score.
7. **Comfort and choice:** Invite, never require, a sensory or creative setting change. Notice whether the child can find and reverse it. Comfort is the outcome; correctness is not a goal for sensory or creative play.
8. **Close:** Ask what they would keep, change, or avoid. Offer a final break and an easy exit. Thank the child without praising compliance or performance.

## Support Levels And Fading

Record one level at a time:

| Level | Facilitator action | Fade rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0. Observe | Wait and watch without hints. | Move on only after a reasonable, unpressured opportunity. |
| 1. Replay | Point to replay or activate the existing instruction channel if the child requests it. | Stop when the child chooses a channel or resumes. |
| 2. Locate | Point to the relevant control or area, without naming the answer. | Remove the point after the child orients to it. |
| 3. Describe | Repeat the product's action in simple words, without solving the task. | Use once, then return control. |
| 4. Demonstrate | Demonstrate one neutral example only when consented to and needed. | Give the device back and use a new example for transfer. |

Do not use hand-over-hand guidance, repeated prompting, praise as pressure, countdowns, or correction loops. If support is still needed, record that honestly; do not convert it into a success.

## Observe, Do Not Judge

Record observable events: "child tapped replay twice", "child used switch to pause", "child moved away after sound", or "child changed motion to still". Do not infer attention, motivation, compliance, intelligence, therapeutic benefit, or diagnosis. No eye contact, stillness, speech, or suppression of harmless self-regulation is a criterion.

Optional off-screen activities, such as choosing between printed cards or describing a preferred control, require separate ethical review, caregiver consent, child assent, and a clear reason. They must never be used to diagnose, grade, or pressure the child, and are not a substitute for in-game validation.
